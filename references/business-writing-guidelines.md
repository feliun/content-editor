# Business Writing Guidelines

> Example reference shipped with the plugin. Replace with your own craft
> guide once you adopt the plugin, or override this file via the
> `business-writing-guidelines` manifest key.

This file defines **how to write well**. The companion `writing-style.md` defines **how the author specifically writes**. Use both for every review. When they conflict, the voice guide wins.

## 1. Why business writing fails by default

Most business writing fails for the same reason: the writer treats words as a wrapper around an idea, instead of treating words as the only thing the reader will see. The reader has no access to the writer's intent. They have access to a string of sentences that either earns the next minute of their attention or doesn't.

Three failure modes account for most of it:

- **Throat-clearing.** Two paragraphs of context the reader never asked for.
- **Vagueness.** Abstract claims with no concrete anchor.
- **Decorative prose.** Sentences that exist because they sound smart.

Each of the following sections is a tool against one of those failure modes.

## 2. Sentence craft

- **One idea per sentence.** If a sentence has two verbs and an "and," it is two sentences pretending to be one.
- **Front-load weight.** Subject, verb, then everything else. Buried subjects make the reader work for free.
- **Vary length on purpose.** A long sentence sets up; a short sentence lands. Three short ones in a row is hammering. Three long ones in a row is fog.
- **Read aloud.** Your ear catches what your eye forgives.

## 3. Word precision

- **Concrete beats abstract.** "We doubled the number of incidents we resolve before the user notices" beats "we improved reliability."
- **Strong verbs beat verb phrases.** "Decide" beats "make a decision." "Cut" beats "remove." "Ship" beats "deliver value."
- **Numbers, names, scenes.** "On a Thursday in March, three customers asked the same question" outperforms "we noticed a pattern."
- **Kill adverbs.** If you reach for "very," "really," "quite," "somewhat," the underlying word is wrong. Replace the word, not the modifier.

## 4. Stickiness (the SUCCESs filter)

A sticky idea passes most of these:

- **Simple.** Reduces to a single sentence the reader can repeat.
- **Unexpected.** Violates a default the reader holds.
- **Concrete.** Made of nouns the reader can picture.
- **Credible.** Backed by a number, a case, or a source they trust.
- **Emotional.** Reaches a stake the reader actually has.
- **Story-shaped.** Has a before and an after.

Run the test before publishing. If you score 0/6, the idea is not finished.

## 5. Structure

The default move is escalation: each section raises the stakes or sharpens the model.

- **Opening.** A claim or a scene, not a setup.
- **Body.** Two to four moves. Each move is a step the reader takes with you.
- **Tension.** The strongest counterpoint, named honestly.
- **Close.** Reframes the opening.

Avoid the symmetric structure where the close just repeats the lead. The reader already knows what you said.

## 6. Remarkability (the Purple Cow test)

If a competitor wrote the same piece, would the reader notice the byline? If no, the piece has no edge. Ways to add an edge:

- A position that costs you something to hold.
- A counter-example most writers omit.
- A number you measured.
- A story the reader has not heard before.

A piece without an edge is content. Content does not get shared, remembered, or quoted.

## 7. Editing rubric

Edit in passes. Each pass has one job.

1. **Cut pass.** Remove 20-30 percent. Targets: throat-clearing, transitions that summarise, qualifier stacks ("I think that maybe"), closing recaps, sentences that exist to sound good.
2. **Concrete pass.** Replace every abstraction with a specific noun, number, or scene.
3. **Verb pass.** Replace every weak verb. Aim for active voice everywhere.
4. **Voice pass.** Read aloud. Does it sound like the author or like a generic LinkedIn post?
5. **Banned-phrase pass.** Search for every entry in the writing-style banned list.
6. **Em-dash pass.** Search for `—` and `--`. Replace.
7. **One-final-cut pass.** Pick one more sentence to remove. There is always one.

## 8. The 12-word test

State the core message in 12 words or fewer. If you can't, the piece does not have a core yet. Either find one or don't ship.

This is the single highest-leverage edit. Most weak pieces are not weak in execution. They are weak because the writer started before knowing what they wanted to say.

## 9. The 10 fatal sins

A draft is rejected on sight for any of these:

1. A banned phrase.
2. An em dash.
3. A motivational close.
4. An opening that asks the reader a rhetorical question they didn't ask first.
5. A claim with no concrete anchor.
6. A passive sentence that obscures who did what.
7. A paragraph that could be cut without losing the argument.
8. An adverb load (more than one per paragraph).
9. A sentence the author wouldn't say out loud.
10. Closing recap that earns no new ground.

These are non-negotiable. Score the rest of the rubric only after these pass.

## 10. Anti-patterns (the writing that doesn't ship here)

- **The corporate update.** "We're excited to announce…": passive, decorative, useless.
- **The motivational sermon.** "Believe in yourself," any version. Always wrong here.
- **The contrarian-for-sport.** Reverses a default with no clearer model underneath.
- **The textbook tone.** Distancing language that pretends the writer has no skin in the game.
- **The open-loop.** A hook with no payoff. The reader feels tricked.
- **The list with no thesis.** Five tips that don't add up to a position.
- **The personal story with no universal hook.** "Yesterday I had coffee with…" with no reason for the reader to care.

## 11. The reader contract

Every piece ships with an implicit contract:

- I will not waste your time.
- I will tell you the conclusion, not pretend it is a surprise.
- I will be specific.
- I will not flatter you.
- I will say what I actually think.

Break any of these and the reader stops trusting the byline. Trust takes a long time to build and one lazy piece to lose.
