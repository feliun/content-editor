# Writing Style

> Example reference shipped with the plugin. The persona below ("Signal") is
> fictional. Replace every section with your own voice once you adopt the
> plugin, or override this file via the `writing-style` manifest key.

The author's voice is **calm, precise, direct**. They write to be useful, not impressive. Every sentence earns its place or gets cut. The reader leaves with a sharper view of the problem, not a bigger opinion of the writer.

This file is the north star. When in doubt, read it again before drafting or editing.

## 1. Voice DNA

- **Calm.** No urgency theatre. No "you need to know this" framing.
- **Precise.** Concrete nouns and active verbs. "We migrated 480 services" beats "we transformed our architecture."
- **Direct.** State the conclusion first. Show the reasoning second. Skip the preamble entirely.
- **Honest.** If something failed, say it failed. If a take is contrarian, defend it instead of softening it.
- **Curious, not contrarian for sport.** Disagreement only when there is a clearer model underneath.

A useful self-test: read the draft aloud. If it sounds like a keynote speech, rewrite it as something you would say across a desk.

## 2. Sentence patterns

- One idea per sentence. Two ideas means a period is missing.
- Lead with the subject. Front-loaded weight scans faster than buried clauses.
- Vary length deliberately. Long, exploratory sentences set context. Short ones land.
- Use parallel structure when listing. Three is the natural rhythm.
- Avoid nested clauses. If a sentence needs more than one comma, split it.

## 3. Word choice

- Concrete over abstract. "Two engineers" beats "the team." "Tuesday" beats "recently."
- Strong verbs over verb phrases. "Decide" beats "make a decision." "Cut" beats "remove."
- Specific examples over generic claims. Numbers, names, dates, scenes.
- Adverbs are flagged for cutting. If you need an adverb, the verb is wrong.
- Jargon is allowed only when the audience knows it cold and a plain word would lose precision.

## 4. Formatting rules (universal, not stylistic)

These apply to every channel.

- **No em dashes.** Use commas, colons, semicolons, periods, or parentheses. Em dashes are a tic that cheapens prose at scale.
- **No emojis.** Anywhere. They signal a brand voice you do not have.
- **No hashtags.** They do nothing for reach in long-form copy and read as marketing.
- **No motivational tone.** No "you've got this," no "let's go," no inspirational closings.
- **No exclamation points.** One per year, maybe.
- **No bold or italic for emphasis in body copy.** If a sentence needs a bold word to land, the sentence is wrong.

LinkedIn-specific:

- **Plain text only.** LinkedIn renders no markdown. Asterisks and underscores show up as literal characters and look amateur.
- **Use line breaks, not headers.** A blank line between two short paragraphs reads as structure.
- **No "link in comments."** Drop the URL inline.

## 5. Structure

Default essay shape:

1. **Lead with the conclusion.** First sentence states the position or the surprise.
2. **Context next.** Why this matters, in two or three sentences.
3. **Reasoning.** Two to four moves that build the argument. Examples per move.
4. **Tension.** The strongest counter you can think of, addressed honestly.
5. **Close.** A single sentence that reframes the lead.

Default LinkedIn shape:

1. Hook (one or two lines).
2. White space.
3. Setup.
4. Pivot or surprise.
5. Payoff.
6. Optional one-line CTA, only if the post genuinely benefits from it.

Skip any of these when the piece doesn't need them. Length is the symptom; clarity is the goal.

## 6. The cut test

Every draft loses 20-30 percent before it ships. Specifically:

- The opening throat-clearing ("In today's world…", "Lately I've been thinking about…").
- The transition sentences that just summarise what was said.
- The qualifier ladder ("I think," "I believe," "in my opinion").
- The closing recap that earns nothing.
- Any sentence that exists because it sounds good.

If the draft can lose a paragraph and the argument still stands, the paragraph wasn't pulling weight.

## 7. The 12-word test

Before publishing, state the core message in 12 words or fewer. If you can't, the piece doesn't have a core yet. Either find it or don't ship.

## 8. Hooks (LinkedIn and newsletters)

A hook earns the second sentence. It does not promise insight; it demonstrates it.

Patterns that work:

- **Concrete claim.** "We deleted 40 percent of our backend tests last quarter. Coverage went up."
- **Reframed default.** "Standups don't fail because they're too long. They fail because nobody decides anything."
- **Confession.** "I was wrong about microservices in 2021, and I'm now sceptical of the way I was right in 2024."
- **Specific scene.** "Tuesday, 9:14 a.m. The deploy is rolling. The on-call's coffee is cold."

Patterns to avoid: rhetorical questions that nobody asks, "raise your hand if…," cliffhangers without payoff, generic surprise ("you won't believe…").

## 9. Banned phrases

These appear nowhere in the author's writing. Any occurrence is treated as a fatal style violation. The list is short enough to memorise.

1. game changer
2. at the end of the day
3. circle back
4. unlock value
5. move the needle
6. low-hanging fruit
7. drink the kool-aid
8. boil the ocean
9. paradigm shift
10. think outside the box
11. take it to the next level
12. let's unpack
13. it's a journey
14. work smarter not harder
15. synergies
16. mission-critical (use "load-bearing" or be specific)
17. world-class
18. best-in-class
19. cutting edge
20. revolutionary

The reason these are banned: every one of them lets the writer skip the work of being specific. They feel like content while transmitting nothing.

## 10. Fatal style violations (auto-fail)

A draft that contains any of the following is rejected and rewritten before it ships, regardless of other strengths:

- A banned phrase from the list above.
- An em dash.
- An emoji or hashtag (unless the user explicitly asked for one).
- LinkedIn body copy with markdown formatting characters.
- Motivational or guru-style closing.
- A sentence the author would not say out loud.

## 11. Audience defaults

Imagine one specific reader: a senior engineer or technical founder, mid-career, allergic to fluff, two browser tabs open. They will give you the first sentence. You earn the second. They have read everything you might cite.

Write to that reader. Anything that doesn't survive their attention budget gets cut.
